# TGP undercuts most of the NZ peptide market

The Gene Protocol's NZD price list is aggressively cheap against every **genuinely NZD-priced** New Zealand competitor found for cosmetic and healing-peptide staples — Melanotan 2 at $60 undercuts Pure Peptides NZ's $165 by **64%**, IGF-1 LR3 100mcg at $65 undercuts the same seller's $135–$170 by **52–62%**, and Tirzepatide 20mg at $150 matches the sticker price New Zealand Peptides charges for half the dose (a **50% per-mg discount**) — but the picture is not uniformly one-sided: two nootropic/mitochondrial products (Semax 10mg, SS-31 10mg) are priced **above** a confirmed NZD-native competitor (Southern Labs), and for a meaningful slice of the catalogue — Glutathione, GLOW, Lipo-C, Cerebrolysin, and several growth-hormone SKUs — no usable NZD pricing exists anywhere in the New Zealand market, making comparison impossible rather than favorable. The research also surfaces a structural trap for anyone benchmarking against "New Zealand" peptide sites: several of the most prominent NZ-branded storefronts (Direct SARMS, Direct Peptides, PharmaLabGlobal, PharmaGrade Store) are international template networks running country-coded subdomains that display USD or GBP pricing, not NZD, so their apparent competitiveness or expense is an artifact of currency conversion rather than a real local price signal. Community and regulatory context sharpens what this all means for TGP: Trustpilot and forum evidence suggests customers value delivery speed and communication over price per se, informal social-media sellers charge a **premium** over registered storefronts rather than undercutting them, and Medsafe, NZ Customs, and Australia's TGA all document a market where mislabeling, contamination, and inconsistent dosing are real and rising risks — meaning price alone is a weak differentiator in a category where trust, COAs, and consistent potency are what buyers say they actually want.

## Two-tier reseller landscape, and a currency trap that inflates or deflates "competitor" prices

New Zealand's peptide-reseller market splits cleanly into two tiers, and confusing them is the single easiest way to misread this whole competitive set. The first tier is genuinely NZ-domiciled and NZD-native: **New Zealand Peptides** (newzealandpeptides.co.nz), **Pure Peptides NZ** (purepeptidesnz.com), **Southern Labs** (southernlabs.co.nz), **BAC Water NZ** (bacwaternz.com, now folded into nzpeptide.com), and **Peptides New Zealand** (peptidesnewzealand.com) all display GST-inclusive dollar prices with no currency ambiguity, and these are the sites that produce the cleanest, most trustworthy comparison points in this report ([New Zealand Peptides](https://newzealandpeptides.co.nz/product/retatrutide/); [Pure Peptides NZ](https://www.purepeptidesnz.com/category/all-products); [Southern Labs](https://southernlabs.co.nz/); [BAC Water NZ](https://bacwaternz.com/)). The second tier looks identical at first glance — **Direct SARMS**, **Direct Peptides**, **PharmaLabGlobal**, and **PharmaGrade Store** all run `new-zealand.` or NZ-branded subdomains stuffed with "buy [peptide] New Zealand" copy — but are in fact a single international retail template deployed across many countries, and their NZ pages inconsistently default to **GBP** (Direct Peptides, PharmaGrade Store) or **USD** (most of Direct SARMS's category pages, though a few of its pages, such as Tesamorelin and HCG, do render natively in NZD, indicating the geo-currency logic is unreliable even within one operator's own site). A 5-Amino-1MQ capsule listing on Direct Peptides' NZ subdomain, for instance, prices at £124.00 — roughly **NZD $289** once converted at ~2.33 GBP/NZD — versus New Zealand Peptides' $95 NZD for a comparable dose, a gap that exists purely because of which currency happened to render on the page a buyer or researcher landed on. Any NZ shopper (or competitor-pricing exercise) that takes a "New Zealand" URL at face value risks either wildly overstating or understating what a local buyer actually pays after FX conversion, card fees, and customs exposure are added on top of the sticker price.

Layered on top of this currency confusion is significant market churn and access friction. **Peptide Lab NZ** and, per its own Trustpilot page, **peptidesnz.com** have both been marked "closed" despite carrying reviews dated into 2026, and outright-defunct domains (**Buy NZ Peptides**, **nzbuypeptides.com**, **nzpeptides.net**) rank in search results for NZ peptide terms while resolving to parked Hostinger pages with no actual storefront ([Trustpilot: Peptide Lab NZ](https://nz.trustpilot.com/review/peptidelab.co.nz); [buynzpeptides.com](https://buynzpeptides.com/)). A further large slice of the nominally active NZ-domain landscape — **PremiumPeps**, **NZ Peptide** (nzpeptide.com), **Cardarine.co.nz**, **PrimePeps**, **Peptides Club**, **PeptideHQ**, and PharmaLabGlobal's NZ subdomain — returned persistent 503/403/409 errors or JavaScript-rendered catalogues that automated research could not price, despite being confirmed to stock the exact SKUs in question. This means the comparison set below leans on a smaller number of resellers than the full universe of "NZ peptide sites" that exist, and several plausible direct competitors (particularly PremiumPeps, which third-party review site Peptide Hub Australia separately rates as a mid-tier, not-the-cheapest vendor at 6.8/10) remain unpriced gaps rather than confirmed comparators ([Peptide Hub Australia](https://peptidehubaustralia.com.au/premium-peps-reddit-reviews/)).

## Trustpilot praises service, not price — and the black market costs more, not less

The community-reputation evidence gathered across every category note tells a consistent story: NZ buyers who leave public reviews care about delivery speed, communication, and problem resolution far more than price, and the loudest recurring complaint — even on well-reviewed sellers — is a desire for independent, verifiable purity testing rather than seller-claimed COAs. **Peptidesnz.com** carries 15 mostly five-star Trustpilot reviews praising "fast service" and support that resolved an issue "within a 24 hour period," but one reviewer explicitly notes, *"Would be nice to see the third party independent results just to confirm the 99% purity"* — a sentiment echoed on **Peptide Lab NZ**'s smaller Trustpilot profile (2 reviews, both 5-star, praising "sweet pricing" and fast rural delivery) despite that company's site having since closed ([Trustpilot: peptidesnz.com](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/peptidesnz.com); [Trustpilot: Peptide Lab NZ](https://nz.trustpilot.com/review/peptidelab.co.nz)). Notably, neither Trustpilot profile nor any other source in this research surfaced a specific complaint naming a registered NZ storefront as guilty of counterfeiting or underdosing — the documented quality failures trace consistently to the informal social-media layer, not the semi-formal .co.nz/.com sellers.

That informal layer is also, counterintuitively, the **expensive** one. 1News's investigation documented a journalist obtaining a Retatrutide 10mg vial via a TikTok/Instagram "DM me" seller for $200 — same-day delivery, no verifiable business identity, and a purity certificate the report calls "six months old" and of "dubious legitimacy," which NZ Drug Foundation infrared testing could not confirm and which may have detected a different substance entirely ([1News](https://www.1news.co.nz/2026/05/17/200-got-me-a-vial-of-peptides-in-minutes-ai-teaches-how-to-inject-it/)). That $200 sits above both New Zealand Peptides' $165 storefront price and TGP's own $110 — direct evidence that anonymity and immediacy carry a price premium in this market, not a discount, upending the common assumption that "black market" automatically means "cheap." Beyond Trustpilot, the richest-looking vein of NZ-specific community sentiment — several active threads on the AU/NZ steroid-and-PED forum **Evolutionary.org**, with titles like "NZ Peptide Source" and "BEST SOURCE FOR PEPTIDES IN New Zealand PLEASE" — proved inaccessible to this research (HTTP 403 on every fetch attempt), leaving only secondhand, unverified search-snippet mentions of suppliers like "AuStarLabs" (said to be recommended) and "NZ Peptides" (said to be viewed skeptically by some members), plus references to a Discord-based seller and a "Life-Forge.com" group chat for "Kiwi members." None of this could be independently confirmed, and it represents the single largest unresolved research gap in the whole project: the community conversation most likely to name specific reputable-versus-overpriced NZ sellers exists but sits behind an access wall this research could not get past.

## Regulators and testing bodies confirm the risk that price alone can't fix

Behind every pricing comparison in this report sits a documented, worsening quality and legal-risk backdrop that should shape how TGP thinks about competing. **Medsafe**, New Zealand's medicines regulator, has issued a formal consumer advisory naming BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, GHRP-6, Ipamorelin, Melanotan II, and Retatrutide specifically as unapproved products "illegally promoted online" that "have not been assessed for safety, quality, or efficacy," may "contain harmful or undisclosed ingredients," carry "the wrong strength," or come from "non-sterile, poor-quality manufacturing" — and the advisory explicitly states that "for research purposes only" labelling is "legally meaningless" under the Medicines Act 1981 ([Medsafe](https://www.medsafe.govt.nz/safety/Alerts/Consumer-advisory-Unapproved-peptide-products-health-warning.asp)). That warning is backed by hard trend data: RNZ reports NZ Customs parcel seizures of suspected peptides and SARMs rose from **15 in 2022 to 370 in the most recent full year, with 200 more already seized this year** — a roughly 25-fold increase — while the NZ Drug Foundation's drug-checking clinics report rising peptide submissions and testing that frequently finds "peptides bought online weren't what they were marketed as" ([RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/health/596283/fears-for-safety-as-influencers-push-synthetic-peptides)). Stuff separately reported a single border seizure of **12,000 illegal peptide vials worth $3 million NZD**, described by authorities as "the tip of the iceberg" ([Stuff](https://www.stuff.co.nz/health/361016203/tip-iceberg-12000-illegal-peptide-drug-vials-worth-3m-seized-nz-border)).

Dosing-accuracy risk is not theoretical either. Australia's TGA tested five seized bottles of Melanotan II nasal spray labelled "30mg" and found actual content ranging from **22mg to 54mg** — up to roughly ±45% variance from the labelled dose — while dermatologists reported via ABC News have observed patients developing new, atypical moles they attribute to the peptide's mechanism combined with uncontrolled product quality ([TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/safety/safety-monitoring-and-information/safety-alerts/melanotan-ii-tanning-peptide-products-found-be-inconsistently-dosed); [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-05/melanotan-ii-peptides-warning-by-specialists-abormal-moles/106749628)). Medsafe treats melanotan the same way as the TGA does — as an unapproved substance requiring formal medicine classification — and separately notes that because many novel peptide derivatives (including unscheduled BPC-157 variants intercepted at the border since 2023) aren't yet formally scheduled, the agency currently lacks legal grounds to seize them outright and instead releases them with a "high-risk medicine letter." Taken together, this regulatory and testing evidence means that a customer's real risk in this category is rarely "did I overpay," and much more often "do I actually know what's in the vial" — which is precisely the question price comparison alone cannot answer, and precisely where TGP's testing, sourcing, and COA practices matter more than its position on any single price table.

## Conclusion: where to lean on price, and where to lean on trust

The pricing evidence supports a clear, honest positioning statement rather than a blanket "cheapest in NZ" claim. TGP is priced aggressively — genuinely 25–65% below confirmed NZD-native competitors — on Melanotan 2, PT-141, Oxytocin, IGF-1 LR3, KLOW, GHK-Cu 100mg, Wolverine, Retatrutide, and Tirzepatide's per-milligram rate, and these are the products worth leading with in any price-based marketing. TGP sits roughly at market on GHK-Cu 50mg, Bac Water 3ml, and (with lower confidence, given currency conversion) NAD+ and Snap8. And TGP is demonstrably **more expensive** than at least one confirmed domestic competitor, Southern Labs, on Semax (29% higher) and SS-31 (42% higher) — a result worth taking seriously rather than dismissing, since it comes from the cleanest, most currency-unambiguous data point available for that category. Finally, a real slice of the catalogue — Glutathione, GLOW, Lipo-C, Cerebrolysin, and several growth-hormone SKUs (Tesamorelin's exact sizes, the standalone CJC1295+Ipamorelin blend, HCG at 10,000 IU) — simply has no usable NZ market comparator, genuine or otherwise; treating these as "we're the cheapest" would be dishonest, and treating them as "unproven demand" would be equally wrong, since in Cerebrolysin's case the absence looks more like a supply gap TGP is positioned to fill than evidence of weak demand.

The deeper lesson from the community and regulatory research is that price competitiveness is necessary but not sufficient in this category. Registered storefronts are being reviewed on speed and support, not on undercutting each other by wide margins (New Zealand Peptides and PremiumPeps differ by only ~7% on Tirzepatide), while the actual premium channel — anonymous social-media sellers — trades on convenience and gets away with unverifiable COAs precisely because no competitor is yet winning customers by publishing real, checkable purity data. Given that Medsafe, Customs, and the TGA have all independently documented a market with rising mislabeling and seizure rates, TGP's most durable competitive advantage is unlikely to be its price list alone; it is more likely to be pairing that already-favorable pricing with visibly verifiable COAs and batch-level testing transparency, which no NZ competitor in this research — cheap or expensive — was found to be doing consistently today.
